The common thread

NESO can see the site, knows where it is, and pays it for what it delivers there

Sources: NESO, RNP Call for Input (Feb 2026), Summary of Feedback (Jul 2026) and OTF (Oct 2026); NESO, DFS updates; DESNZ, RNP Delivery Plan

Let’s make this concrete for a typical customer: an industrial site with a combined heat and power unit, or a battery, or both. Each of NESO’s balancing reforms changes something for that site. Lowering the BM threshold, phased in from 2029 and reaching assets of 1 megawatt by 2032, means a few megawatts of on-site plant appears on NESO’s screens. That matters for constraints, because NESO can only pick a site for its location if it can see it. Fifteen-minute settlement pays for fast, accurate response, which is exactly what a battery or a modern CHP controller gives. Requiring FPNs to match traded positions, if it goes ahead, makes the site’s plan a firm baseline, which is what any constraint action is measured and paid against. Unit bidding, tying bids to individual units in known places, is still being assessed and is further off, but it’s the change that lets a well-placed site compete directly for constraint actions. And some locational buying is already here: the Demand Flexibility Service buys by zone and buys constraint actions, the Local Constraint Market pays sites behind the Scottish boundary, and government is trialling levy-free power for demand in constrained areas. The common thread is that NESO can see the site, knows where it is, and pays for what it delivers there.
