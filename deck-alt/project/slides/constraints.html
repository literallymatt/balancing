This is the root of the constraint problem, and it links the two halves of the timeline we saw earlier. Up to Gate Closure, the wholesale market trades on a single national price, as if the network between buyer and seller had no limits. Nothing checks whether a trade can physically be delivered. So a Scottish wind farm can sell, say, 1,000 megawatts to a supplier in the south, and both sides submit FPNs that match. At Gate Closure NESO inherits those plans. But in real time the boundary between Scotland and England can only carry, in this example, 600 megawatts. NESO’s own analogy is a motorway network where some stretches get congested, and the Scotland to England boundary is the classic one. So NESO uses the Balancing Mechanism twice. It accepts bids to turn the wind farm down by 400 megawatts behind the constraint, paying it for lost output and subsidy. Then it accepts offers to turn generation up by the same amount in front of it, usually gas in the south, near demand. We effectively pay twice for one megawatt-hour, and every pound of that flows through BSUoS to suppliers and on to our customers. It isn’t only Scotland: during the June heatwave NESO was managing network constraints in the South East of England and restricting interconnector flows to do it. And flexible demand can play on both sides of this: turning up behind a constraint to use the surplus wind, or turning down in front of it instead of gas turning up.
