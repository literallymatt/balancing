So, to pull it together: how does demand side response from our industrial and commercial customers help NESO manage the grid? Three ways, matching the problems we’ve seen. First, energy balancing. When margins are tight, at a cold, still evening peak, a site that cuts load or runs its own CHP or battery saves NESO from buying expensive gas offers, whose average accepted price doubled in the past year. Today that’s paid through the Demand Flexibility Service, the Capacity Market, or BM offers through a virtual lead party. With RNP, 15-minute settlement means fast, accurate response is paid for properly instead of being averaged away over half an hour. Second, and increasingly the big one, constraints. A site behind a constraint, say in Scotland on a windy night, can turn up and use wind that would otherwise be paid to switch off. A site in front of one, in the South East during a heatwave, can turn down instead of gas turning up. Today that’s the Local Constraint Market, DFS constraint actions and BM bids, but demand earns just 0.3% of the turn-down money while wind gets 94%. Under RNP, NESO is launching a long-term Demand for Constraints market, with a tender this year and delivery from 2028, and government is trialling levy-free power for demand in constrained areas. Third, visibility. Most of our customers’ sites are below the BM threshold today, so NESO can’t see them and has to hold extra margin for the unknown. The reforms make assets visible from 1 megawatt and bring new ones into the BM from 10, so sites that are already metered, controllable and signed up will be first in line. And the bottom line: NESO itself says these reforms make constraints cheaper to manage but don’t remove them. So NESO will keep paying someone to move, and a visible, controllable site in the right place is exactly that someone.
