Flexibility

One site, either side of a constraint

Our example site: 2 MW of CHP, a 1 MW battery and some flexible process load, metered and controllable

Behind a constraint

e.g. Scotland on a windy night: too much power

  • Site does: turns the CHP down and imports instead; charges the battery
  • Paid through: a BM bid, the Local Constraint Market, or DFS turn‑up
  • Why it wins: CHP saves gas by turning down, so it can bid far cheaper than wind, which NESO must pay to stop

In front of a constraint

e.g. South East with imports limited: too little

  • Site does: runs the CHP harder, discharges the battery, eases process load
  • Paid through: a BM offer or DFS turn-down
  • Why it wins: it sits where the power is needed, competing with gas offers that have doubled in price

Paid

MWh moved from the site’s plan × the accepted price, shared between the customer and its aggregator

Here’s how that plays out for one site: say 2 megawatts of CHP, a 1 megawatt battery and some process load that can move, all metered and under automatic control. Which side of a constraint it sits on depends on where it connects to the network. If it’s behind a constraint, say in Scotland on a windy night, the problem is too much power, so NESO wants less generation or more demand there. The site turns its CHP down and imports from the grid instead, and charges its battery. It gets paid through a Balancing Mechanism bid via its aggregator, the Local Constraint Market, which NESO runs for sites behind the Scottish boundary, or a DFS turn-up event. And it has a real edge: a CHP that turns down saves the gas it would have burned, so it can bid much more cheaply than a wind farm, which NESO has to pay to stop because it loses its subsidy. If the site is in front of a constraint, say in the South East when imports are limited, it’s the reverse. NESO needs more power locally, so the site runs its CHP harder, discharges its battery and eases off process load. It gets paid through a BM offer or a DFS turn-down event, competing against gas offers whose average accepted price has doubled in a year. Either way, the payment is the megawatt-hours it moved away from its plan multiplied by the accepted price, shared between the customer and the aggregator that bids it in.
