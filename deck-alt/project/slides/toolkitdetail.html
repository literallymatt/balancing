The Balancing Mechanism

Offer

Paid to increase output or reduce demand

Bid

Reduce output or increase demand. Wind is paid to do this; gas pays NESO back.

Frequency response

Dynamic Containment, Moderation, Regulation

Reserve

Quick, Slow and Balancing Reserve

Constraint tools

Trades and DFS constraint actions

Interconnectors

Trades and export restrictions

Demand Flexibility

Businesses and homes paid to shift use

Last resort

Margin notices, emergency instructions

Source: NESO, Review of Electricity System Operation during Extreme Heat (Sept 2026), section 5.7 and Appendix C

How does NESO do it? The main tool is the Balancing Mechanism. Every BM unit, generators, batteries, interconnectors and some demand, submits prices for moving away from its FPN. An offer is a price to increase output or reduce demand. A bid is a price to reduce output or increase demand. Wind farms bid negative prices, so NESO pays them to turn down, because they lose output and subsidy; a gas plant turning down actually pays NESO back because it saves fuel. NESO accepts the cheapest actions that also work for the network, which is why location matters so much. Alongside the BM, NESO buys frequency response and reserve ahead of time, runs specific constraint tools such as trades, trades across interconnectors, and uses the Demand Flexibility Service, which since November 2024 has been a year-round tool NESO calls when it is cheaper than the alternatives. When none of that is enough it escalates to margin notices and emergency instructions, which is what we saw in the June heatwave.
