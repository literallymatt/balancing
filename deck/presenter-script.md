## Slide 1 · Cover (1:00)

### Balancing costs are rising fast, and flexibility is part of the answer

**Broad points**
- This talk is about money: the cost of keeping the GB grid balanced.
- Four parts: how fast costs are rising, what NESO does and why, the reforms on the way, and where our customers' flexibility fits.
- The BM analysis is our own, from Elexon's public data. Everything else is sourced on the slides.

**Detail**
- Open with the hook: every pound NESO spends balancing the grid ends up on our customers' bills through BSUoS, and that number has gone from £1.8bn in 2020 to about £3.4bn so far this year, heading for about £4.3bn.
- Set the frame you'll return to at the end: NESO's problem is increasingly about *where* power is, not just *how much*, and flexible demand in the right place is one of the cheapest fixes.
- Keep it short. Don't read the subtitle out.

## Slide 3 · Annual balancing costs (3:00)

### 2026 costs reached £3.4bn by October, 1.9× 2020's £1.8bn, heading for about £4.3bn

**Broad points**
- £1.1bn in 2018, £1.8bn in 2020, £4.2bn at the 2022 gas-crisis peak.
- The blue part is constraints: £0.6bn in 2018, £2.1bn in 2025, which is 70% of the year, and the share is still climbing.
- 2026 is at about £3.4bn with two months to go: nearly double 2020, and more than three quarters of it is constraints. On NESO's forecast the full year is about £4.3bn, 2.4 times 2020 and level with the 2022 peak.
- The orange bars are NESO's forecasts, not our data: the last two months of 2026 (taking the year to about £4.3bn), about £4.2bn for 2027, and a possible peak near £8bn by 2030.

**Detail**
- Walk the chart left to right. Point at 2022 and explain it was a *price* spike: reserve and other services got expensive during the gas crisis. It wasn't that NESO needed to do more balancing.
- Then point at the blue segments growing. Define constraint costs once, simply: the money NESO pays because the network can't carry power from where it's made to where it's used.
- 2026: about £3.4bn spent by the end of October, and September and October alone cost over £1.1bn. Add NESO's own forecast for November and December and the year lands around £4.3bn, level with 2022's £4.2bn. Part of the autumn jump is gas prices; the constraint volume is a network problem that stays when prices fall. Don't call 2026 a record: it's within the estimate's margin of 2022 and of NESO's 2027 forecast.
- Forward look: NESO's October BSUoS forecast puts 2027 at about £4.2bn. NESO hasn't published annual numbers for 2028 and 2029, which is why there's a gap. Its 2025 Annual Balancing Costs Report says costs could peak around £8bn by 2030 as wind connects faster than the network is reinforced, and that about £4bn of that is avoidable if key network projects come forward.
- One honest caveat, said once and quickly: the bars are on the same basis as NESO's headline figures (its Monthly Balancing Services Summary, which gives £2.7bn for 2024/25, as NESO reports). That summary runs to August, so September and October are NESO's early figures plus a small estimate and 2026 may move a little. Treat the forecasts as direction of travel.
- Land the message: constraints are the story, and constraints are a location problem.

## Slide 4 · Energy vs system balancing (2:00)

### NESO balances for two reasons: how much power, and where it is

**Broad points**
- **Energy balancing:** total supply equals total demand every second, so frequency holds near 50 Hz.
- **System balancing:** power flows stay within each line's limits, and voltage and stability stay in range, in each area.
- Some tools do both jobs (BM, trades, DFS), but they're chosen for different reasons.
- Flexibility has proven itself on the energy side; the newer, bigger prize is on the system side.

**Detail**
- Energy side, use the examples on the card: wind under forecast (BM offers, reserve); a big station trips (frequency response within seconds); a cold, still evening peak (trades ahead, DFS turn-down).
- System side: a windy night with the Scotland to England boundary full (bid wind down, offer gas up in the south, pay flexible demand in Scotland to turn up through DFS); a heatwave that congests the network in the South East, as in June (local actions, interconnector limits); low demand with little spinning plant (stability and voltage contracts).
- Make the link back to slide 3: the blue bars, the ones exploding, are system balancing.
- Bridge: "So when does NESO actually do all this? It depends where you are in the timeline."

## Slide 5 · The market trades; NESO makes it physically work (2:00)

### Up to an hour ahead it's the market's job; after that it's NESO's

**Broad points**
- Suppliers and generators trade to match their own position, from years ahead right up to Gate Closure.
- An hour ahead, at Gate Closure, each BM unit locks its Final Physical Notification (FPN).
- In real time units run to their FPN unless NESO instructs otherwise. Afterwards Elexon charges imbalance per half-hour.
- NESO acts throughout, but its main tool, the Balancing Mechanism, opens at Gate Closure. Costs go to suppliers via BSUoS.

**Detail**
- Read the grid by row. Top row is the market: trade, lock the plan, deliver, settle. Bottom row is NESO.
- Make clear NESO isn't idle before Gate Closure: it forecasts demand and wind, buys reserve, response and some constraint contracts ahead, and in the hours before it can trade wholesale and across interconnectors and call DFS.
- But the Balancing Mechanism, the dark box, is where most of the money goes.
- Finish on the two physical rules at the bottom: supply must equal demand every second, and flows must stay within network limits even after a fault. The market doesn't see either of these. That's the reason NESO exists as a residual balancer.

## Slide 6 · How the Balancing Mechanism works (3:00)

### NESO buys its way back to a workable plan, one half-hour at a time

**Broad points**
- Units declare: their plan (FPN), their limits (MEL and MIL), how fast they move, and prices to move up or down.
- **Offer** = more power on the system (generate more or use less). **Bid** = less power (generate less or use more).
- NESO starts from the cheapest action, then filters for location, speed and duration.
- Pay-as-bid; Elexon settles; costs go to BSUoS, which since 2023 falls entirely on demand.

**Detail**
- The slide shows only the offer and bid boxes and the timeline strip. Talk through what units declare, how NESO picks and pay-as-bid out loud; those points are no longer on the slide.
- The key point on bids: a gas plant turning down usually pays NESO back because it saves fuel. Wind bids negative, because turning down loses it output and subsidy, so NESO pays it to stop. That's why constraint costs grow with wind.
- Emphasise that the cheapest action isn't always taken. A cheap unit in the wrong place, or too slow, loses to a dearer one that works. Location matters.
- Timeline strip, three phases for one half-hour period T:
  - **Before Gate Closure (before T−60):** units trade and can still change their plans and their bid and offer prices.
  - **Gate Closure to end of period (T−60 to T+30):** FPNs fix each unit's plan, and prices are locked. NESO accepts bids and offers, mostly close to real time. If asked: limits (MEL, MIL, ramp rates) can still be redeclared after Gate Closure.
  - **Settlement (days later):** Elexon pays accepted actions, cashes out imbalance, and the cost goes to BSUoS.
- Plant the P342 point now, because slide 11 comes back to it: since 2017, parties can keep trading and notifying contracts to Elexon right up to the start of the half-hour. Those trades change who's long or short for settlement, but not what units physically do, because the plans NESO works from are already locked.
- Customer angle in one line: BSUoS sits entirely on demand, so this is our customers' money.

## Slide 8 · The market trades as if the wires were unlimited (2:30)

### One national price means NESO pays twice to undo trades the network can't carry

**Broad points**
- Before Gate Closure: Scottish wind sells 1,000 MW to a southern supplier. One national price, no network check, so it becomes both FPNs.
- Real time: the boundary only takes 600 MW.
- NESO pays wind to turn down 400 MW, and pays gas in the south to turn up 400 MW. Paid twice.
- It isn't only Scotland: in June's heatwave NESO was managing network constraints in the South East and restricting interconnector flows.

**Detail**
- This is the most important slide of the section. Slow down.
- Use NESO's own analogy: a motorway network where some stretches get congested. The Scotland to England boundary is the classic jam.
- Walk the left panel, then the Gate Closure line, then the right panel. The volumes are illustrative.
- Make "paid twice" land: we pay the wind farm for energy it doesn't produce, and pay a gas plant for energy it does, and all of it reaches bills through BSUoS.
- Bridge from the hidden REMA slide, briefly: "The textbook fix is zonal pricing, so that power in Scotland is cheaper when the wires are full. Government looked at that under REMA and ruled it out in July 2025. It's keeping one national price and calling the replacement Reformed National Pricing, or RNP: build in the right places, cut constraint costs, and reform balancing."
- Plant the flexibility idea: flexible demand can help on both sides. Turn up behind the constraint to use surplus wind, or turn down in front of it instead of gas turning up.

## Slide 11 · NESO's balancing reforms (2:30)

### Five reforms make redispatch cheaper, but none of them removes the congestion

**Broad points**
- **Shorter settlement:** 15 minutes, with 5 minutes tested. Sharper price signals.
- **Lower BM threshold:** full BM from 30 MW (existing) and 10 MW (new); visibility for new generation, including batteries, from 1 MW. NESO sees and can dispatch more assets.
- **Trading stops at Gate Closure:** deadline back to T−60, reversing P342.
- **FPNs match trades:** checked at portfolio level, within tolerances. Targets open positions and NIV chasing.
- **Unit bidding and dispatch reform:** still being assessed.
- NESO's own verdict: cheaper to manage, but the congestion stays.

**Detail**
- This is the balancing pillar of RNP, which NESO leads. Status is as at NESO's Operational Transparency Forum on 28 October.
- Two reforms are fairly settled in direction: shorter settlement and lower thresholds.
- Two had industry pushback and are less certain: the trading deadline (this is the P342 point from slide 6) and FPN matching.
- One sits in the background: NESO isn't running a full cost-benefit analysis on unit-level bidding yet and will recommend on it alongside dispatch reform at the end of 2027; dispatch proposals will be shared "in due course".
- Bridge the hidden visibility slide in one sentence when you reach the threshold row: "NESO estimates around 33 GW of distributed assets, more than three quarters, are neither visible to it nor available for real-time balancing. Lowering the threshold is about fixing that."
- Read the bottom line out word for word. It's the bridge to flexibility: something still has to absorb surplus in the north and fill gaps in the south.
- For the detail behind each reform (why 10 MW, why 15 minutes, why P342 is controversial), see `notes/rnp-reforms-qa-brief.md` and the Q&A prep. Don't go into it unless asked.

## Slide 12 · Reform timeline (1:30)

### A decision in 2027, changes from 2029, fully in by the early 2030s

**Broad points**
- Deciding: Call for Input (Feb to Jul 2026, 81 responses), Expert Panel since April, cost-benefit analysis in October, NESO recommends to DESNZ and Ofgem in December.
- NESO only recommends. Ofgem and the Secretary of State decide, with no date set, so realistically 2027.
- If approved: tolerances consulted 2027 to 2028; first go-live Q1 2029 (new assets over 30 MW into the BM); 2030 (new over 10 MW); 2032 (all over 1 MW, existing sites included, with visibility rather than bidding below 10 MW; and 15-minute settlement for wholesale and large sites); 2035 (15-minute for homes and small businesses).
- These are NESO's planned dates, not decisions.

**Detail**
- Keep this one brisk. Top row is "nearly done", bottom row is "if it happens".
- Why settlement goes in two steps: it follows the meters. Large sites already have half-hourly Code of Practice metering; homes and small businesses need upgrades first.
- The customer message: nothing changes overnight, but by the early 2030s a multi-megawatt site is likely to be visible to NESO, and possibly in the BM, whether it chooses to be or not. Getting ready early is the opportunity.

## Slide 14 · How I&C flexibility helps NESO, and gets paid for it (2:30, then questions)

### Flexible demand is the visible, controllable resource in the right place that NESO will keep paying for, and RNP opens more ways to get paid

**Broad points**
- **Energy balancing, "steps down at the peak":** today DFS turn-down (from 0.1 MW), Capacity Market, BM offers via a VLP. With RNP, 15-minute settlement from 2032 pays for fast, accurate response instead of averaging it away.
- **Constraints, "moves on the right side":** today DFS constraint actions (which replaced NESO's Local Constraint Market on 7 October) and BM bids, but demand earns 0.3% of turn-down pay and wind 94%. With RNP, the Demand for Constraints market (delivery from 2028) and the levy-free trial pay for location, and unit-level bidding, if adopted, could follow.
- **Visibility, "a site NESO can see":** today most sites are below the BM threshold, so a VLP is the only way in. With RNP, new assets join the BM from 10 MW (2030) and new on-site generation and batteries share data from 1 MW (2032). NESO can only pick a site it can see.
- **Not all upside:** trading ending at Gate Closure and FPNs having to match trades both raise imbalance risk. A site that can flex within the period helps manage it.
- Bottom line: reform won't remove congestion, so NESO will keep paying visible sites in the right place to move.

**Detail**
- Tie each column back to an earlier slide: energy balancing to slide 4, constraints to slide 8, visibility to the threshold row on slide 11.
- Energy: a site that cuts load or runs its own CHP or battery at a tight evening peak saves NESO from buying BM offers, usually gas, and the average accepted offer price roughly doubled over the past year (about £121 to £245/MWh, our BM analysis).
- Constraints is the big one: wind gets about 94% of the turn-down money and demand about 0.3%, so there's a lot of room for demand to compete. NESO's Demand for Constraints market (new flexible demand in Scotland, up to 10-year contracts) started procurement in October, with contracts due in April 2028 and delivery from 2028, and government is trialling removing final consumption levies for demand in constrained areas (parts of Scotland and the East of England).
- Visibility: a metered, controllable site is exactly what NESO says it's short of. Be precise about the thresholds: full BM from 10 MW is for *new* assets (existing ones only from 30 MW, and only if connected under G99). The 1 MW tier is new generation, which includes batteries and CHP, and it means sharing data, not bidding, so it earns nothing by itself. Demand-only sites under 10 MW aren't covered and keep using an aggregator or VLP. Our view (not NESO's words) is that sites already metered, controllable and signed up will be easiest for NESO to pick.
- Dates: say them out loud, and say they're NESO's planned dates, not decisions.
- Unit-level bidding: NESO recommends on it, with dispatch reform, at the end of 2027, and it was the least popular reform in the Call for Input. If adopted, it lets NESO pick units by location before Gate Closure, which is what lets a well-placed site compete head-on with wind.
- Not all upside: ending trading at Gate Closure (reversing P342) and FPN matching are costs for suppliers like us; NESO says traders "will need to finalise trades earlier and manage higher imbalance risk". Both drew industry pushback, and FPN matching is the least certain of the five. But if they happen, a customer site that can flex within the period is worth more, because it helps manage that risk. "Demand for Constraints" and the levy-free trial sit alongside NESO's balancing reforms rather than being part of them, so don't call them "the reforms".
- Close by summarising the talk in four lines:
  1. Balancing costs are rising fast, and constraints are now almost all of it.
  2. The causes are structural: a network that hasn't kept up, one national price that ignores it, and assets NESO can't see.
  3. Zonal pricing is off the table; NESO's reforms help, but by its own account they won't remove congestion.
  4. Our customers' flexibility is a direct answer, and the routes to be paid for it are opening up.
- "Happy to take questions."
