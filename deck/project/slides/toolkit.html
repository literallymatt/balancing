02 · NESO’s role

How NESO balances: energy and system

Energy balancing

Matching supply and demand

  • Purpose: keep total supply equal to demand every second, so frequency holds near 50 Hz
  • Markets: wholesale trading up to Gate Closure, BM bids and offers, reserve (Quick, Slow and Balancing Reserve), frequency response and the Demand Flexibility Service

System balancing

Managing grid conditions

  • Purpose: keep power flows within network limits, and voltage and stability in range, in the right places
  • Markets: BM bids and offers chosen by location, constraint trades and intertrips, the Local Constraint Market, DFS constraint actions and interconnector trades

The Balancing Mechanism

Sits across both

Offer: paid to increase output or reduce demand. Bid: paid to reduce output or increase demand. Constraints mean bidding wind down and offering gas up, so system actions drive the rising bill.

Source: NESO heat review (Sept 2026), section 5.7 and Appendix C (NESO order of actions); NESO balancing services and Local Constraint Market pages

NESO’s actions fall into two kinds, and NESO labels them this way itself. Energy balancing is about quantity: making total supply equal total demand every second, so frequency stays near 50 hertz. Most of that is done by the market before Gate Closure, through wholesale trading. After that NESO tops it up with BM bids and offers, holds reserve such as Quick, Slow and Balancing Reserve, buys frequency response to catch sudden swings, and uses the Demand Flexibility Service. System balancing is about where the power is: keeping flows on each line within its limit, and keeping voltage and stability right in each area. That is inherently locational, so NESO picks BM actions by where the unit sits, and uses constraint trades, intertrips, the Local Constraint Market, DFS constraint actions and interconnector trades. The Balancing Mechanism sits across both. An offer pays a unit to increase output or reduce demand; a bid pays it to reduce output or increase demand. Wind farms bid negative prices, so NESO pays them to turn down because they lose output and subsidy, while a gas plant turning down pays NESO back. When a constraint binds, NESO bids wind down behind it and offers gas up in front of it, and that system balancing is where the cost growth is. When none of this is enough, NESO escalates to margin notices and emergency instructions, which is what we saw in the June heatwave. Keep this split in mind: flexibility has already proven itself on the energy side, and the newer, bigger prize is on the system side.
