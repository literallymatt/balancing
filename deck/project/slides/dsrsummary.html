So, to pull it together: how does demand side response from our industrial and commercial customers help NESO manage the grid, and where do the reforms open new ways for customers to get paid? Three ways, matching the problems we’ve seen. First, energy balancing. When margins are tight, at a cold, still evening peak, a site that cuts load or runs its own CHP or battery saves NESO from buying expensive BM offers, usually gas, and the average accepted offer price doubled in the past year. Today that’s paid through the Demand Flexibility Service, which now takes sites from 0.1 megawatts, the Capacity Market, or BM offers through a virtual lead party. With RNP, 15-minute settlement, due from 2032 for large sites, means fast, accurate response is paid for properly instead of being averaged away over half an hour. Second, and increasingly the big one, constraints. A site behind a constraint, say in Scotland on a windy night, can turn up and use wind that would otherwise be paid to switch off. A site in front of one, in the south when the boundaries from Scotland are full, can turn down instead of gas turning up. Today that’s DFS constraint actions, which replaced NESO’s Local Constraint Market in October, and BM bids, but demand earns just 0.3% of the turn-down money while wind gets 94%. Next, NESO’s Demand for Constraints market for new flexible demand in Scotland started procurement in October, with delivery from 2028, and government is trialling levy-free power for demand in constrained areas. Further out, and only if NESO adopts it, unit-level bidding would let NESO pick sites by location before Gate Closure, which is what lets a well-placed site compete head-on with wind. Third, visibility. Most of our customers’ sites are below the BM threshold today, so the only way in is through a virtual lead party, and NESO has to hold extra margin for what it can’t see. The reforms bring new assets into the BM from 10 megawatts from 2030, and from 2032 new on-site generation and batteries from 1 megawatt have to share data with NESO. That tier is data, not bidding, and demand-only sites under 10 megawatts aren’t covered, so they’ll still go through an aggregator. But NESO can only pick a site it can see. These are NESO’s planned dates, not decisions. It isn’t all upside. Ending trading at Gate Closure and making FPNs match traded positions both raise imbalance risk for suppliers like us, and both drew pushback from industry, so they’re among the less certain reforms. But if they happen, a customer site that can flex within the period becomes more valuable, because it helps manage that risk. And the bottom line: NESO itself says these reforms make constraints cheaper to manage but don’t remove them. So NESO will keep paying someone to move, and a visible, controllable site in the right place is exactly that someone.
