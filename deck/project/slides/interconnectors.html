02 · NESO’s role

Interconnectors: how NESO changes the flow

Cable owners auction capacity to traders, so the market sets the flow first. NESO then has three ways to move it:

1

SO-to-SO trades

Bilateral deals with the system operator at the other end, such as RTE, TenneT, Elia or Statnett, agreed close to real time

2

Trades with the market

NESO’s trading desk buys or sells cross-border energy with traders who hold interconnector capacity

3

Emergency actions

Emergency assistance between operators, and export restrictions when GB margin is tight

For energy

More import when GB is short, or export when it is long

For the system

Less import where the cable lands behind a constraint, as in the South East during the June heatwave

Source: NESO heat review (Sept 2026), section 5.7 and Appendix C; NESO trading and interconnector pages

Interconnectors deserve a slide of their own, because they sit across both kinds of balancing. NESO doesn’t own interconnector capacity or run the auctions for it. The cable owners sell capacity to traders, mostly in explicit auctions since Brexit, and traders schedule flows based on the price difference between GB and the neighbouring market. So, like everything else, the market sets the flow first. NESO then has three ways to change it. First, SO-to-SO trades: bilateral agreements with the system operator at the other end, for example RTE in France or TenneT in the Netherlands, which can be agreed close to real time. Second, trades with the market: NESO’s trading desk buys or sells cross-border energy with counterparties who hold capacity. Third, in an emergency, operators can call on each other for emergency assistance, and NESO can restrict exports when GB margins are tight. Any of these can be done for energy, importing more when GB is short, or for the system, importing less where a cable lands behind a constraint. During the June heatwave NESO was managing import constraints into the South East, so the right answer there was fewer imports, not more.
