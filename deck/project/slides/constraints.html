England and the south

Where most of the demand sits

1. Bid: turn down behind the constraint

NESO pays wind farms in the north to reduce output they could otherwise have sold

2. Offer: turn up in front of it

It then pays plant in the south, usually gas, to replace that energy near demand

Source: NESO, Review of Electricity System Operation during Extreme Heat (preliminary report, Sept 2026), section 5.4 and 5.7

Before the numbers, a quick reminder of what a constraint is. NESO’s own analogy is a motorway network: some stretches get congested. The classic example is the boundary between Scotland and England, which doesn’t have enough capacity to carry all the wind generated in Scotland south to where the demand is. When that happens, the market has already decided who generates, but physics says the power can’t all get there. So NESO uses the Balancing Mechanism twice: it accepts bids to turn wind down behind the constraint, compensating the wind farm for lost output and subsidy, and then accepts offers to turn generation up on the other side, usually gas in the south. We effectively pay twice for one megawatt-hour. It’s not only Scotland: during the heatwave NESO was managing import constraints into the South East of England. And note that flexible demand can play on both sides of this: turning up behind a constraint, or turning down in front of it.
