Why does NESO need to balance at all? In GB’s market design, suppliers and generators trade with each other right up to an hour before delivery, each trying to match their own customers’ demand or their own output. At Gate Closure, an hour ahead, every large unit submits its Final Physical Notification: what it intends to generate or consume. From that point NESO takes over as what the design calls the ‘residual balancer’. Two physical rules force its hand. Supply and demand must match every second, or frequency moves away from 50 hertz; NESO aims to keep it between 49.8 and 50.2. And power flows must stay within what the network can carry, even if a line trips. The market doesn’t see either of those things. Afterwards, Elexon settles everyone in 30-minute periods, charging imbalance to anyone who didn’t match their contracted position. Keep that 30-minute period and the FPN in mind, because both come back when we talk about reform.
