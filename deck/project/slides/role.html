Why does NESO need to balance at all, and when does it act? In GB’s market design, suppliers and generators trade with each other right up to an hour before delivery, each trying to match their own customers’ demand or their own output. At Gate Closure, an hour ahead, every large unit locks in its Final Physical Notification: what it intends to generate or consume. Afterwards, Elexon settles everyone in half-hour periods, charging imbalance to anyone who didn’t match their contracted position. NESO isn’t idle until Gate Closure. Well ahead, it forecasts demand and wind, and buys reserve, frequency response and some constraint contracts. In the hours before, its trading desk can buy or sell in the wholesale market and across interconnectors, and it can call the Demand Flexibility Service. But its main tool only opens at Gate Closure: the Balancing Mechanism, where it accepts bids and offers to move units away from their plans. That’s where most of the money goes, and it all ends up on bills through BSUoS. Two physical rules force NESO’s hand. Supply and demand must match every second, or frequency moves away from 50 hertz. And power flows must stay within what the network can carry, even if a line trips. The market doesn’t see either of those things.
