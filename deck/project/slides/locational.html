Why do constraints cost so much? A big part of the answer is that GB has a single national wholesale price. Power traded for delivery in the north of Scotland costs the same as power in Kent, so the market draws up its schedules as if the network between them had no limits. NESO then has to undo a large share of those schedules in real time. NESO itself says it sometimes redispatches more than half of national demand, and in its February call for input it said plainly that it is no longer the residual balancer the market design intended; it has become a de facto central scheduler. A telling example: NESO looked at four batteries in the north and found 59% of their BM actions were just cancelling their own market schedules. The national price told them to charge or discharge in ways that made congestion worse, and NESO paid to reverse it. Constraint costs have quadrupled since 2018, from about half a billion pounds to £2.1 billion last year. Without a locational signal, the market keeps creating problems that NESO pays to fix.
